What is CAR-T cell therapy?

CAR-T stands for Chimeric Antigen Receptor T-cell therapy — a revolutionary form of immunotherapy that re-engineers your own immune cells to fight cancer.

Module 1~8 min
🎯 The bounty hunter analogy

Your T-cells are police officers who can't recognise a criminal (cancer cell) in disguise. CAR-T therapy gives each officer a new biometric scanner — the Chimeric Antigen Receptor — that sees through every disguise. Now they can identify and eliminate the target on sight.

The 4 core steps

Step 1

Collect — leukapheresis

Blood is drawn and passed through an apheresis machine. T-cells are extracted; remaining blood is returned. Takes 3–6 hours at a specialist apheresis centre.

Step 2

Engineer — viral transduction

In a sterile lab, a harmless viral vector inserts the CAR gene into each T-cell's DNA. 40–70% of cells receive the gene. These are now CAR-T cells.

Step 3

Grow — bioreactor expansion

CAR-T cells multiply ~1,000× over 7–10 days in a bioreactor under carefully controlled temperature, pH, and cytokine conditions.

Step 4

Infuse — back to the patient

Cells are frozen, passed QC, shipped to hospital, and infused in under 30 minutes. CAR-T cells then multiply inside the patient and hunt cancer cells.

Autologous vs allogeneic

Autologous ("from self")

Made from the patient's own cells. Every batch is unique. Currently the regulatory gold standard. Lower rejection risk but requires 2–3 weeks to manufacture.

Custom per patientLow rejection

Allogeneic ("off-the-shelf")

Made from a healthy donor. One batch serves multiple patients. Faster access, lower cost. Still largely in clinical trials — graft-versus-host disease must be engineered away.

Donor cellsFaster delivery
🍕 Analogy — pizza

Autologous = a hand-crafted pizza made specifically for you from your own ingredients. Allogeneic = a premium frozen pizza made in bulk — faster and cheaper, but not perfectly tailored to every preference.

FACT / JACIE site accreditation

Before a hospital can administer CAR-T therapy, it must be accredited by FACT (US/international) or JACIE (Europe). Accreditation certifies that the centre has the clinical expertise, facilities, quality systems, and data collection required to safely administer cell therapy.

Module 14Mandatory gating step
⭐ The Michelin star analogy

FACT/JACIE accreditation is like a Michelin star for cell therapy centres. It's not just a certificate — it's a rigorous inspection by experts who verify that the kitchen (clinical team), the menu (protocols), the ingredients (products), and the service (patient care) all meet the highest standard. Without it, you can't be listed on the map.

FACT vs JACIE — what's the difference?

FACT (Foundation for the Accreditation of Cellular Therapy)

US-based, internationally recognised. Developed the first standards for haematopoietic cell therapy. FACT standards are used in the US, Canada, Australia, and many other countries.

US standardInternational

JACIE (Joint Accreditation Committee ISCT-Europe & EBMT)

European body. Joint venture between ISCT (International Society for Cell & Gene Therapy) and EBMT (European Blood and Marrow Transplantation Society). Standards are harmonised with FACT but have EU-specific elements.

EU standardEBMT aligned

The accreditation process

Application & self-assessment

Centre completes a detailed self-assessment against the FACT/JACIE Standards (Clinical Programme, Collection Facility, Processing Facility). APD (Accredited Programme Director) takes overall responsibility.

Document review

SOPs, quality manual, outcome data, personnel training records, and facility descriptions submitted electronically. Typically takes 3–6 months to prepare.

On-site inspection

Team of peer inspectors visits the centre (2–3 days). Reviews facilities, interviews staff, observes procedures, and checks records. Findings documented as deficiencies (minor / major / critical).

Deficiency resolution

Centre responds to deficiencies with corrective actions. Critical deficiencies must be resolved before accreditation is granted. Major deficiencies require follow-up within 3 months.

Accreditation granted

Certificate issued for 3 years. Annual reporting of outcomes (patient numbers, response rates, adverse events) required to maintain accreditation.

What accreditation covers

Clinical programme

Patient selection criteria, informed consent, treatment protocols, outcome data collection, adverse event management, follow-up programme.

Collection facility

Apheresis equipment, staff training, collection procedures, labelling and chain of identity, product handling and storage.

Quality management

SOPs, deviation management, CAPA, staff training records, outcome tracking, continuous improvement processes.

Facility & equipment

Cleanroom or controlled environment (if processing on-site), equipment calibration and maintenance, cryostorage if applicable.

⏱️ Timeline reality: A new treatment centre typically takes 12–18 months from starting the application process to achieving accreditation. This lead time is a critical input to commercial launch planning.

Market access & reimbursement

Getting a CAR-T product approved by regulators is only half the battle. Market access — getting payers to fund it for patients — is often harder. At $350K–$500K per treatment, CAR-T faces the most intense payer scrutiny of any medicine.

Module 13Commercial-critical
🏘️ The planning permission analogy

Regulatory approval is like getting an architect's certificate that the building is safe. Market access is like getting planning permission from the local council — and reimbursement is the bank agreeing to fund it. Each gatekeeper has different criteria, and you need all three before anyone can move in.

The market access journey

Regulatory approval (FDA/EMA)

BLA/MAA granted. Product can legally be marketed. Does not guarantee payment or patient access.

Health technology assessment (HTA)

Independent bodies (NICE in UK, IQWiG in Germany, HAS in France) evaluate clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness. They decide whether the product offers sufficient value at the proposed price.

Price & reimbursement negotiation

Sponsor negotiates with national payers or insurance companies. For CAR-T, outcome-based contracts (OBC) are increasingly used — the manufacturer refunds part of the price if the patient doesn't respond.

Formulary listing & coverage

Payer adds the product to their formulary (approved list). Treatment centres can then seek reimbursement for eligible patients.

Individual patient prior authorisation

For each patient, the treatment centre submits a prior authorisation request to the payer. The patient coordinator manages this — often the longest step in real-world access.

Key HTA bodies

Outcome-based contracts (OBCs)

Because CAR-T is expensive and not all patients respond, payers increasingly demand outcome-based contracts. The manufacturer only receives full payment if the patient achieves a defined outcome.

Pay-for-performance model

Payment tied to response at Day 30 or 90. If patient achieves CR (complete remission), full price paid. If partial or no response, a rebate is applied. Requires real-world outcome data collection.

Annuity / instalment model

Payment spread over 5 years, stopping if the patient relapses. The manufacturer bears the financial risk of early relapse. Used for Novartis Kymriah in some markets.

Managed access scheme

Patients access the treatment while further evidence is collected (e.g. NICE's CDF — Cancer Drugs Fund in UK). Final reimbursement decision made after 2–3 years of real-world data.

ℹ️ HEOR role: Health Economics and Outcomes Research (HEOR) teams build the value dossier — the clinical and economic evidence package submitted to HTA bodies. They model cost-effectiveness using quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and negotiate the evidence standards with payers.

Patient enrollment & COIC tracking

Chain of Identity and Custody (COIC) is the documented thread linking a specific patient to their cells at every step. One gap in this chain and the batch cannot be released.

Module 4Compliance critical
🛂 The passport control analogy

COIC is like international travel. Your passport is checked and stamped at every border crossing (each custody transfer). If a stamp is missing — even one — you cannot board the next flight. The entire journey is traceable and auditable.

Patient enrollment — step by step

1

Physician referral & eligibility check

Oncologist identifies patient as a CAR-T candidate. Checks diagnosis, prior treatments, organ function, and performance status. Contacts the treatment centre.

2

Insurance/payer authorisation

For commercial slots: prior authorisation from the payer is required before any slot is booked. This can take days to weeks. Patient coordinators manage this process.

3

Slot request submitted

The treatment centre submits a formal slot request to the manufacturer (or sponsor's cell therapy team). Includes patient ID, diagnosis code, product requested, and preferred apheresis date.

4

Slot confirmed & apheresis scheduled

Manufacturing confirms the slot week. The apheresis centre is given a target collection date. Pre-apheresis patient preparation begins (e.g. stopping certain medications).

5

Apheresis performed — COIC begins

Blood is collected. The first COIC document is generated: the apheresis label and collection form. Every subsequent handoff must reference this unique patient identifier.

6

Manufacturing completes — CoA issued

Batch passes QC. CoA is generated. The patient's carers are notified of the expected delivery date. Lymphodepletion chemotherapy is timed to precede infusion by 2–7 days.

COIC — Chain of Identity & Custody

COIC tracks the identity of whose cells these are (identity) and who held them at each moment (custody). Click any transfer point to see what documentation is required.

⚠️ COIC failure consequences: If any custody transfer cannot be verified, the batch is quarantined. The patient may receive wrong-patient cells if controls fail — an outcome with potentially fatal consequences. COIC is non-negotiable.
🧬 Analogy — organ transplant matching

Just as an organ transplant requires blood type and tissue matching documents at every stage — from donor harvest to recipient surgery — COIC ensures the cell product stays linked to the right patient from vein to vein, with no opportunity for mix-up.

Slot management

A slot is a reserved window of manufacturing capacity for one patient batch. Getting slot allocation right is what keeps patients on schedule.

Module 5Simulator
✈️ The airline seat analogy

Slots are seats on a flight. Clinical trial patients, commercial patients, non-patient runs, and buffer reserves each need seats. Some passengers are VIPs (clinical — strict SLA), some economy (commercial), the back row is reserved for emergencies (buffer). Once the plane is full, no one else can board.

Clinical trial slots

Reserved for patients enrolled in Phase I/II/III investigational studies. Governed by IND/CTA, GCP, and the trial protocol.

Who uses it?

Patients enrolled in a clinical trial. Identified by trial ID (not Rx). Slot count per arm pre-agreed with sponsor.

Governance

IND/CTA · GCP · clinical protocol · chain of identity with trial ID. Deviations trigger protocol amendment review.

Convertible?

No — clinical slots are locked to the trial arm. Cannot be reassigned to a commercial patient.

🔬 Research grant analogy

A clinical slot is like a funded research grant — ring-fenced for a specific purpose (the trial), governed by strict rules, and not redirectable to another use.

Commercial slots

For patients receiving a market-approved CAR-T product. Governed by BLA/MAA, cGMP, and a commercial SLA.

Who uses it?

Patients with a physician prescription for an approved product (e.g. Kymriah, Yescarta). Payer authorisation required first.

Governance

BLA/MAA · cGMP · commercial supply agreement · SLA (guaranteed turnaround, e.g. 21 days from receipt).

Convertible?

Limited — tied to specific approved product and payer contract terms.

🍽️ Restaurant reservation analogy

A commercial slot is a reserved table at a Michelin-star restaurant. The kitchen has committed to serving you by a specific time (SLA). Your name is on the booking — it can't just be given to someone else.

Non-patient slots

Manufacturing runs not intended for patient administration — validation, training, development, comparability.

What they're used for

Process validation, stability studies, tech transfer, operator training, scale-up batches, comparability batches using surrogate leukapheresis.

Key distinction

No patient identity is linked. Never administered to a patient. Scheduled separately to avoid capacity conflicts.

Convertible?

Yes — with approval, can be converted to a patient slot in urgent situations.

🏋️ Training session analogy

Like a sports team's training session — the pitch is booked, the players are working hard, but there's no actual match happening. The capacity is consumed but no patient receives treatment.

Buffer slots

Reserved capacity held open — no patient assigned — to absorb failures, surges, and urgent compassionate use cases.

Why they exist

Manufacturing can fail. Cells may not expand. QC may fail. An urgent compassionate use case may arrive. Buffer = safety net for rapid re-scheduling.

How many?

Typically 10–20% of monthly capacity. A policy decision balancing patient safety risk against commercial efficiency.

Convertible?

Yes — this is their entire purpose. Converted to Clinical or Commercial when triggered by the slot management team.

🚑 A&E bay analogy

Buffer slots are like empty bays kept open in an emergency department. You could fill them with elective patients — but you hold them back because you know the ambulance will arrive. Reserved space = rapid emergency response.

QC sites & EU qualified person release

In the European Union, every batch of an advanced therapy medicinal product (ATMP) must be certified for release by a Qualified Person (QP) — a legally responsible individual registered with the national regulator.

Module 8EU regulation
⚖️ The notary analogy

A QP is like a notary public for medicines. They personally review all the documents, confirm the batch meets every legal requirement, and put their professional registration number on the line when they certify release. If the batch causes harm and the QP signed it off incorrectly, they face personal regulatory action.

What is QP release?

Under EU Directive 2001/83/EC and the ATMP Regulation 1394/2007, no batch of a medicinal product can enter the EU market without QP certification. For CAR-T, this means:

Batch manufactured & QC tested

All in-process and final release tests completed at the manufacturing site or designated QC site.

Batch record compiled

Full manufacturing batch record (MBR), all QC certificates, deviation reports (if any), and supply chain documentation assembled into a batch dossier.

QP review

The designated QP reviews the complete dossier. They verify compliance with the Marketing Authorisation (MAA), GMP, and any country-specific requirements. This can take 1–3 days for a typical ATMPs batch.

QP certification

QP signs and dates the batch certificate. The product is released from quarantine. Shipment to the treatment centre is authorised.

QC sites by EU country

Many EU countries require QC testing or QP release to be performed within national borders or by a nationally-registered QP. Click a country to learn more.

Import testing requirements

For batches manufactured outside the EU (e.g. in the US), additional import testing is often required:

Sterility re-test

Many EU QPs require a repeat sterility test on the imported batch, even if it passed in the US. This adds 7–14 days — a significant challenge for time-critical CAR-T products.

Identity testing

Product identity must be confirmed on the EU-side sample. Flow cytometry panels may differ between US and EU specifications.

Abbreviated testing (ATMP waiver)

Hospital exemption (Article 28) allows certain ATMPs to be released without full import testing if manufactured for a named patient and certified by the hospital pharmacy QP. Reduces timeline by up to 10 days.

⏱️ Key challenge: For fresh CAR-T (no cryopreservation), the 24–48 hr transit window means QP review must be pre-staged — the QP begins reviewing draft documents before the batch even finishes manufacturing.

Out-of-spec orders & deviations

When something goes wrong in the manufacturing or logistics process, it triggers a structured escalation pathway. Understanding this is critical for everyone in the chain.

Module 7Critical process
✈️ The aviation analogy

Out-of-spec orders are like aircraft technical issues before takeoff. Every issue is categorised (minor snag vs flight-critical fault), documented, escalated to the right authority, and resolved before the aircraft can depart. The system is designed so nothing gets missed — and nothing gets waved through.

What triggers an out-of-spec order?

The escalation pathway

1 — Detect

Out-of-spec result identified

A QC test, in-process check, or logistics deviation raises a flag. The time of detection and the exact parameter out of range are documented immediately.

2 — Quarantine

Batch placed on hold

The product is quarantined — it cannot be shipped until the deviation is resolved. A Deviation Report is opened in the quality management system.

3 — Investigate

Root cause analysis

Quality, manufacturing, and sometimes the sponsor investigate the root cause. Is it a one-off? A systemic issue? Will it affect product safety or potency?

4 — Decide

Disposition decision

Three possible outcomes: Release (deviation is minor, risk-benefit favourable), Re-test (repeat the failed test), or Reject (batch cannot be used — patient re-slot required).

5 — CAPA & close

Corrective action & patient re-scheduling

A CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action) is raised to prevent recurrence. If batch is rejected, the slot management team activates a buffer slot and the patient coordinator is notified immediately.

Impact on the patient & slot team

Patient impact

If batch is rejected: patient must undergo a second leukapheresis (repeat cell collection). This delays treatment by 3–6 weeks and is physically taxing. In rapidly progressing disease, this can be critical.

Slot team response

Buffer slot is activated. The rejected patient is placed at the top of the wait list for the next available slot. Communication to the hospital must happen within hours of the rejection decision.

ℹ️ Key metric: Manufacturing failure rates in CAR-T are approximately 5–15% depending on patient health status, cell quality, and product type. Buffer slot sizing is calibrated to absorb this.

Bridging therapy

Between leukapheresis and CAR-T infusion, a patient can wait 14–21 days. For patients with aggressive cancers, this wait can be dangerous — bridging therapy keeps the disease controlled while the drug is being manufactured.

Module 9Critical timing
🚧 The roadworks analogy

Bridging therapy is like a temporary diversion while the main road is being rebuilt. The patient's cancer is still active during manufacturing. Bridging keeps the cancer "in check" — stopping it from progressing so far that the patient is no longer eligible to receive CAR-T when it arrives.

Why bridging therapy is needed

CAR-T is approved for patients who have relapsed or are refractory after multiple prior therapies. Their disease is often aggressive and can double in size within days. A 14–21 day manufacturing window is long enough for the cancer to progress to the point of making the patient unable to receive CAR-T.

⚠️ If bridging therapy is too aggressive, it can affect T-cell quality in the patient, potentially impacting CAR-T product performance. The oncologist must balance disease control against preserving T-cell function.

Bridging options

Low-dose chemotherapy

Most common approach. Drugs like bendamustine, gemcitabine, or reduced-intensity CHOP. Aims to slow disease without exhausting the patient before infusion.

Steroid therapy

Dexamethasone or prednisone used to rapidly reduce tumour burden and inflammation. Short-acting, can be stopped before lymphodepletion begins.

Targeted therapy / immunotherapy

Where available, targeted agents (ibrutinib, venetoclax) or checkpoint inhibitors. Selected based on the patient's tumour biology and what they've received previously.

Radiation therapy

Focal radiotherapy for bulky lesions causing symptoms (e.g. spinal cord compression). Localised — does not affect the T-cells.

Bridging & the slot management interface

Slot confirmed → bridging decision

Once the manufacturing slot is confirmed, the oncologist decides whether bridging is needed and which agent. This decision is documented in the patient's case notes and shared with the slot manager.

Mid-bridging check

Typically at Day 7–10, a re-staging scan or assessment checks if the patient's disease is stable. If the patient has worsened significantly, the clinical team may escalate — triggering communication to the slot manager about potential slot change.

Bridging washout period

Bridging agents are stopped 3–7 days before lymphodepletion to allow clearance. Timing is coordinated with the expected drug product delivery date.

Lymphodepletion begins

Typically fludarabine + cyclophosphamide (Flu/Cy), given 2–7 days before CAR-T infusion. This is the formal start of the infusion phase.

Infusion day & acute care

CAR-T infusion is a short procedure — but the weeks following require intensive monitoring for two serious toxicities: Cytokine Release Syndrome (CRS) and ICANS. Every clinical team must be trained and prepared before administering.

Module 10REMS requiredLife-critical

The infusion process

Drug product arrives at hospital pharmacy

Pharmacist verifies CoA, chain of identity, patient match, and drug product integrity. Batch is released for dispensing.

Thawing (if cryopreserved)

Product thawed in 37°C water bath at bedside. Takes ~5 min. Must be infused within 30–60 min of thaw. No washing typically required (DMSO tolerated at infused volumes).

Bedside verification

Two-nurse check of patient ID, product label, and dose. The most critical COIC verification point — wrong patient at this step means wrong-patient infusion.

Infusion

IV infusion over 30 min maximum. Patient monitored with continuous vitals. Pre-medication (acetaminophen + antihistamine) given to reduce acute reactions.

Observation — minimum 14 days

Patient must remain near the treatment centre for 14 days post-infusion, available for daily assessment. This is mandated by REMS in the US.

Cytokine Release Syndrome (CRS)

CRS occurs when CAR-T cells multiply and attack cancer, releasing a massive wave of cytokines (immune signalling proteins). This causes a systemic inflammatory response. Timing: typically Day 1–14 post-infusion, peak Day 3–7.

🌡️ The wildfire analogy

CRS is like a controlled burn that gets out of control. The CAR-T cells are fighting cancer (the burn is doing its job) but the immune system response generates so much heat that it starts damaging healthy tissues too. Tocilizumab (anti-IL-6) is the fire-retardant dropped from above — it doesn't stop the CAR-T cells working, but dampens the inflammatory signal.

ICANS — Immune effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome

ICANS is a neurological toxicity affecting the brain and nervous system. Can occur concurrently with CRS or after it resolves. Managed with corticosteroids (dexamethasone). Timing: typically Day 4–14, can occur later.

⚠️ Clinical flag: ICANS can present subtly — tremor, word-finding difficulty, confusion. Daily neuro assessment (ICE score) is mandatory. Rapid escalation to Grade 3–4 can occur within hours.

REMS — Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (US)

All FDA-approved CAR-T products in the US require a REMS programme. REMS restricts who can prescribe, dispense, and administer CAR-T.

Treatment centres must:

  • Be REMS-certified (enrol with the manufacturer)
  • Have tocilizumab on-site before each infusion
  • Be able to provide ICU-level care
  • Enrol patients in the REMS programme
  • Submit adverse event reports via REMS portal

Consequences of REMS non-compliance

A centre that loses REMS certification cannot receive or administer CAR-T. Patients must be re-referred. This makes REMS compliance a top priority for treatment centre teams and a key check in FACT/JACIE accreditation.

Post-infusion monitoring & follow-up

The CAR-T journey doesn't end at infusion. Response assessment, long-term follow-up, and managing late effects continue for months to years. Gene therapy products like CAR-T require 15 years of follow-up under FDA guidance.

Module 11Up to 15 years follow-up

Response assessment schedule

Day 1–30

Acute monitoring

Daily assessment for CRS and ICANS. Blood tests (CRP, ferritin, cytokine panel). ICE neurological score daily. Resolution of acute toxicity documented.

Day 28–30

First response assessment

PET-CT or CT scan. Response categorised as Complete Response (CR), Partial Response (PR), Stable Disease (SD), or Progressive Disease (PD). This is the primary endpoint in most trials.

Month 3 & 6

Durability assessment

Repeat imaging and blood tests. CAR-T cell persistence measured by PCR or flow cytometry. B-cell aplasia (marker of ongoing CAR activity) monitored — IVIG supplementation if prolonged.

Year 1–5

Annual follow-up

Yearly imaging for responders. Monitoring for late effects: prolonged cytopenias, secondary malignancies, infections (due to B-cell aplasia). Neurological assessments in patients who had ICANS.

Year 5–15

Long-term gene therapy follow-up

FDA requires 15-year follow-up for all gene therapy products. Annual contact with patient or physician. Monitoring for insertional oncogenesis (extremely rare — reported in 0 commercial CAR-T patients to date).

Managing CAR-T relapse

Approximately 30–50% of patients who initially respond eventually relapse. There are two main patterns:

CD19-negative relapse (antigen escape)

Cancer cells lose the CD19 target that the CAR recognises. The CAR-T cells can no longer see the cancer. Options: different CAR-T targeting a different antigen (e.g. CD22), bispecific antibodies, or allogeneic CAR-T.

CD19-positive relapse (CAR-T exhaustion)

Cancer still expresses CD19 but CAR-T cells have become exhausted or lost persistence. Options: second CAR-T infusion (re-infusion), checkpoint inhibitors to reinvigorate CAR-T cells, or allogeneic CAR-T.

Late effects & quality of life

Prolonged B-cell aplasia

CD19-targeted CAR-T depletes normal B-cells as well as cancerous ones. Patients need monthly IVIG infusions to prevent infections. Can last months to years.

Cytopenias

Low blood counts (red cells, platelets, neutrophils) can persist for months. G-CSF support, transfusions, and dose-modification of any post-CAR medications required.

Neurological late effects

Some patients who experienced ICANS report cognitive effects ("chemo fog"), concentration difficulties, or fine motor changes months later. Neuropsychological support recommended.

Pharmacovigilance & adverse event reporting

Pharmacovigilance (PV) is the science of detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects of medicines. For CAR-T, PV is particularly demanding due to the novel and serious nature of the toxicities.

Module 12Regulatory obligation
🛡️ The air traffic safety analogy

Pharmacovigilance is like aviation incident reporting. Every near-miss, every adverse event — however minor — is reported into a central system so patterns can be spotted before a catastrophic event occurs. The benefit of the system depends on everyone reporting, even when it's inconvenient.

REMS programme — US-specific

All six currently approved CAR-T products in the US are covered by REMS programmes (managed separately by each sponsor: Novartis, Kite/Gilead, BMS/Celgene). Key REMS obligations:

Sponsor obligations

  • Certify all treatment centres
  • Train centres on CRS/ICANS management
  • Ensure tocilizumab availability
  • Submit REMS status reports to FDA
  • Track and report REMS programme outcomes

Treatment centre obligations

  • Complete REMS training annually
  • Have tocilizumab on-site before each infusion
  • Enrol patients in the programme
  • Report adverse events via REMS portal
  • Maintain ICU/resuscitation capability
ℹ️ Risk Management Plan (RMP): The EU equivalent of REMS. The RMP documents the known and potential risks, the evidence about them, and risk minimisation measures (e.g. educational materials, restricted distribution). Submitted as part of the MAA and updated periodically.

The pharmacovigilance team

👩‍⚕️ PV physician / QPPV

The Qualified Person for Pharmacovigilance (QPPV) is the EU-designated individual responsible for establishing and maintaining the PV system. Must be permanently available to regulatory authorities. A legal appointment, like the QP for manufacturing.

📋 PV case processor

Receives incoming adverse event reports, assesses them for seriousness and causality, and enters them into the safety database (Argus, ARISg). Ensures timely submission to regulators within legal timelines.

🔬 Medical safety officer

Reviews safety data for signals — patterns suggesting a new or increased risk. Prepares Benefit-Risk assessments. Interfaces with clinical, regulatory, and commercial teams on label changes triggered by safety findings.

Demand forecasting & S&OP

Sales & Operations Planning (S&OP) is the process that aligns commercial demand signals with manufacturing capacity. In cell therapy, this is especially complex because each slot consumes significant resources and the lead times for inputs (viral vectors, kits) are months long.

Module 15Strategic planning
✈️ The airline scheduling analogy

S&OP in cell therapy is like an airline planning its flight schedule 18 months out. You need to know how many routes (trial arms + commercial markets) you'll fly, how many seats per flight (slots per month), how much fuel you need (viral vector), and how many pilots are available (trained manufacturing staff). Get it wrong and planes sit empty — or worse, patients are left waiting on the runway.

The S&OP cycle

Demand planning (monthly)

Commercial teams forecast patient demand by market, product, and time horizon. Inputs: trial enrolment rates, commercial prescription trends, new treatment centre activations, market access approvals in new countries.

Supply review (monthly)

Manufacturing reviews available slot capacity against planned demand. Checks viral vector inventory and lead times, kit supply, staffing levels, and any planned maintenance downtime.

Pre-S&OP meeting

Cross-functional review: supply chain, manufacturing, clinical ops, commercial, regulatory. Gaps between demand and supply are identified and options presented for decision.

Executive S&OP

Senior leadership approves the capacity plan, resolves conflicts (e.g. whether to prioritise clinical or commercial slots if capacity is tight), and sets buffer slot policy for the coming quarter.

Slot schedule published

Approved slot allocations published to the slot management system. Treatment centres receive their slot windows. Manufacturing schedules are locked.

Key demand inputs

Trial enrolment signals

How many patients per month are being enrolled across each trial arm? What is the screen failure rate? Are new sites being activated? Clinical ops teams provide these numbers monthly.

Commercial prescription trends

Market data, CRM prescription tracking, and key account feedback. New treatment centre approvals and REMS certifications trigger demand step-ups. Market access decisions in new countries create demand spikes.

Viral vector supply forecast

CDMO batch schedules, yield variability, and inventory levels. Viral vector is the longest-lead input (2–6 months) and most expensive (~$400–500K/batch). A vector shortage cascades directly to patient slot delays.

⚠️ Bullwhip effect: Small changes in commercial demand forecasts can cause large swings in manufacturing schedules and vector orders — amplified through the supply chain. S&OP discipline is what dampens this effect in cell therapy supply chains.

Technology transfer & process changes

Technology transfer is the process of moving manufacturing from one site to another — or from a development process to a commercial one. It's one of the most technically demanding activities in cell therapy operations, requiring regulatory approval for every significant change.

Module 16Highly regulated
🍽️ The recipe transfer analogy

Tech transfer is like moving a Michelin-starred restaurant to a new building — in a different city, with a different kitchen, a different team, and slightly different ingredients. The dish must taste identical. You can't just hand over the recipe; you have to train the new chefs, validate the new kitchen equipment, prove the dish is the same, and get a food inspector to sign off before you open to customers.

Types of technology transfer

Clinical to commercial

Moving from a research-grade or Phase I/II manufacturing process to a fully validated, scalable, commercial-grade cGMP process. Often involves scale-up, automation, and process optimisation.

Site-to-site transfer

Moving manufacturing from one GMP facility to another — e.g. from a CDMO to an in-house facility, or to a second commercial site for capacity expansion. Requires comparability studies.

Process change management

Any change to the approved manufacturing process — new equipment, new reagent source, modified step — requires formal change control. Post-approval changes need regulatory notification or approval depending on their impact.

The tech transfer process

Transfer plan & gap analysis

Document current process (process description, batch records, analytical methods). Identify gaps at the receiving site: equipment, staff capabilities, raw material sourcing differences.

Training & knowledge transfer

Sending site scientists train receiving site staff. SOPs adapted for the new facility. Equipment at the receiving site qualified (IQ, OQ, PQ — Installation, Operational, Performance Qualification).

Engineering runs

Non-patient runs at the new site to demonstrate the process can be executed. Results compared to historical data from the sending site. Issues identified and resolved.

Comparability study

Formal demonstration that the product made at the new site is comparable to product from the sending site. Tests cover identity, purity, potency, and safety. Regulatory acceptance of comparability is required before the new site can supply patients.

Regulatory submission & approval

Process changes submitted as a Prior Approval Supplement (PAS) or Changes Being Effected (CBE-30) in the US; Type II or Type IA/IB variation in the EU. Approval unlocks the new site for patient supply.

⏱️ Timeline: A full site-to-site tech transfer for CAR-T typically takes 18–36 months from initiation to regulatory approval. This is a key input to long-term capacity planning.

Digital systems & IT integration

Cell therapy operations are underpinned by a stack of interconnected software systems. Understanding how data flows between them — and what happens when they fail to integrate — is essential for anyone working in operations, quality, or supply chain.

Module 17Data integrity

The cell therapy IT stack

Data integrity requirements

All computerised systems used in GMP environments must comply with 21 CFR Part 11 (US) and EU GMP Annex 11. These regulations require:

ALCOA+ principles

  • Attributable — who did it?
  • Legible — can it be read?
  • Contemporaneous — when was it done?
  • Original — first capture
  • Accurate — no errors
  • + Complete, Consistent, Enduring, Available

Audit trail requirements

Every GMP system must maintain a complete, time-stamped audit trail of all entries and modifications. Who changed what, when, and why. Audit trails cannot be deleted — only flagged as errors with a reason.

Electronic batch records (eBMR)

The shift from paper batch records to electronic MBRs (eBMR) eliminates transcription errors, enforces process steps in sequence, and speeds up QP review. FDA and EMA both encourage eBMR adoption.

Integration challenges

System silos

CTMS, LIMS, ERP, and slot management systems are often from different vendors and don't natively communicate. Manual re-entry of data between systems creates transcription error risk and delays.

Validation burden

Every computerised GMP system must be validated (IQ/OQ/PQ). Any software update requires re-validation. This creates a tension between keeping systems current and maintaining a validated GMP state.

API integration

Best-practice solution: API integrations between systems so data flows automatically and in real time. Critical interface: CTMS to slot system (patient enrolment triggers slot request), LIMS to ERP (QC release triggers shipment initiation).

Commercial launch readiness

Transitioning from a clinical trial programme to a commercial product is one of the most complex operations in biopharma. For CAR-T, first patient treated (FPT) is the finish line — but it requires months of parallel preparation across every function.

Module 18Cross-functional
🚀 The rocket launch analogy

Commercial launch is like a rocket launch. Every system — medical affairs, supply chain, commercial, regulatory, quality, IT — must be checked and ready simultaneously. You can't launch with one system failing. And unlike a product recall of a tablet, you cannot pull a CAR-T patient mid-treatment.

The launch readiness checklist

Treatment centre on-boarding

Centre identification & targeting

Medical affairs and commercial teams identify which hospitals have the patient volume, expertise, and infrastructure to become authorised treatment centres.

REMS / QP certification

Centre submits to REMS (US) or completes QP documentation (EU). Staff complete mandatory training on CRS/ICANS management and product-specific protocols.

FACT/JACIE accreditation (if not already held)

Centres without accreditation must initiate the 12–18 month accreditation process. Launch planning must account for this lead time.

System onboarding

Centre staff trained on the manufacturer's patient ordering portal, slot request system, and COIC documentation requirements. System access tested end-to-end.

First patient treated

Typically accompanied by intensive support from the manufacturer — on-site medical science liaison (MSL), logistics coordinator, and medical affairs contact available 24/7 for the first 5–10 patients.

Patient outcomes & real-world evidence

Clinical trial results tell us what CAR-T can do under ideal conditions. Real-world evidence (RWE) tells us what it actually does in routine clinical practice — with older patients, more comorbidities, and less stringent eligibility criteria. RWE is increasingly required for reimbursement decisions.

Key RWE data sources

EBMT registry (Europe)

European Blood and Marrow Transplantation registry. Treatment centres submit outcome data for all CAR-T patients. Largest source of real-world CAR-T data in Europe. Used for JACIE accreditation outcomes and HTA submissions.

CIBMTR (US)

Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research. US registry collecting outcomes for all cell therapy patients. Mandatory data submission for FACT-accredited centres. Critical for FDA post-market commitments.

PASS (Post-Authorisation Safety Studies)

EMA-mandated studies that collect safety data on approved products in routine practice. Sponsors commit to PASS at the time of MAA approval. Results fed back to EMA annually for benefit-risk re-assessment.

CAR-T relapse mechanisms — understanding failure

Antigen escape (target loss)

Cancer cells downregulate or lose the CD19 antigen the CAR recognises. The CAR-T cells become blind to the cancer. Estimated 30–40% of relapses. Addressed by dual-targeting CARs (CD19+CD22) in development.

CAR-T cell exhaustion

CAR-T cells become functionally exhausted over time — still present but unable to proliferate or kill. Driven by inhibitory receptor upregulation (PD-1, LAG-3). Checkpoint inhibitors may help reinvigorate exhausted CAR-T cells.

CAR-T cell fratricide

Rare: the CAR accidentally targets a protein on the T-cells themselves, causing the CAR-T cells to kill each other during manufacturing. Being designed out in next-generation protocols.
